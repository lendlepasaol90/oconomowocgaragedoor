How Sections Work

Your Door Is Built In Separate Sections

Most garage doors in Oconomowoc are sectional doors. Instead of one big slab, the door is made of four or five horizontal sections, sometimes more on taller doors, joined by hinges so it can bend around the curve of the track. When homeowners say panel, they usually mean one of these full-width sections. That design is good news when something gets damaged, because a single bad section can often be swapped out without replacing the whole door.

Each section has its own role. The bottom section carries the bottom brackets and the lift cables, and it takes the most abuse from water, salt and bumpers. The top section holds the top brackets and the opener arm. The middle sections are mostly about strength and looks, though on insulated doors each one also contributes to the door's overall thermal performance. Knowing which section is damaged tells us a lot about how involved the replacement will be.

Within a section there are details that matter for matching. Steel doors come in different gauges and with or without insulation. The face may be smooth, woodgrain embossed or stucco textured. The pattern could be short raised panels, long raised panels, flush, or a carriage-house design with decorative overlays. Window sections add glass style and frame shape to the list. Every one of these has to line up for a replacement section to look like it belongs.

Finding A Match

Tracking Down The Right Replacement Section

The hardest part of panel replacement is usually not the installation. It is finding a section that matches. We start by identifying the manufacturer and model. Many doors have a label on the inside edge of a section or on the end stile, and the hardware, section height, rib pattern and thickness tell us a lot even when the label has faded or peeled off. With that information we can check whether the exact section is still available.

For doors that are still in production, ordering a matching section is fairly simple, and the new piece usually looks right once it is in place. Color is the one thing to plan for. A white door that has been in the sun for ten years has faded slightly, so a fresh section can look a shade brighter for a while. Painted and stained finishes can be blended, and many homeowners decide to paint the whole door so every section is uniform.

Discontinued doors are trickier. Manufacturers change designs, embossing patterns and section heights over the years, and older models eventually drop out of their catalogs. In some cases a close match from the same or another manufacturer will work, and in others there is simply nothing that will fit the existing hinges and look right. When that happens we tell you so before you spend money, and we walk through your options.

  • Section height and thickness measured
  • Face texture and panel pattern matched
  • Insulation type and steel gauge confirmed
  • Window style and glass checked for top sections
  • Color and finish differences explained up front

Types Of Damage

Dents, Cracks, Rot And Rust Through

Not every damaged section needs to come out. Small dings in a steel door are mostly cosmetic, and they rarely affect how the door works. We start getting concerned when a dent is deep enough to crease the steel, when it sits along a hinge line or the edge of a section, or when the section has bowed so the door no longer closes square. Those dents can stress the hinges, make the door bind and let water and cold air in.

Cracked sections are common on older doors with thin steel and on composite or fiberglass doors that took a hard hit in the cold. A crack near a hinge or bracket mounting point is a structural problem, because the hardware can tear out. Wood doors have their own issues. The bottom section of a wood door that sits in snowmelt every winter can soften and rot, and once rot gets into the frame of the section it spreads faster than most people expect.

Rust is the other big one. Steel doors are galvanized and painted, but once the paint is chipped and salty slush sits on the bottom edge, rust starts working through. Surface rust can sometimes be treated. Rust that has eaten holes through the bottom of the section, or that has weakened the area where the bottom bracket mounts, means that section should be replaced. We check those mounting points closely because they hold the cables that carry the door's weight.

What To Look For

Signs A Section Needs Replacing

Get A Free Quote

Deep Dent Or Crease

A dent that creases the steel, sits along a hinge line or bends the edge of the section is more than cosmetic. It can make the door bind in the track, stress hinges and leave gaps where cold air and water get in.

Cracks Near Hardware

Cracks around hinge or bracket mounting points mean the hardware may tear loose. On the bottom section that hardware holds the lift cables, so a crack there is a safety concern and should be looked at soon.

Rust Holes Along Bottom

Salt and slush sit against the bottom of the door all winter. Bubbling paint and rust that has eaten through the steel means the section is losing strength, especially where the bottom bracket attaches to the door.

Soft Or Rotted Wood

On a wood door, press gently on the bottom rail. Wood that feels spongy, flakes apart or shows dark staining has likely started to rot. Rot spreads, and a weakened section can let hardware pull free under load.

Door Will Not Close Square

If a section is bowed after an impact, the door may stop closing evenly, leaving a wedge-shaped gap at the floor. The opener may also reverse or strain as the bent section drags through the track curve.

Broken Or Fogged Windows

Window sections with cracked glass, missing inserts or seals that have failed and fogged over can sometimes be fixed by replacing the glass. When the frame or section around the window is damaged, replacing the section works better.

Repair Or Replace

When A New Door Is The Smarter Spend

Replacing one section is usually a good value when the rest of the door is in decent condition, the model is still available, and only one section is affected. Things change when two or more sections are damaged. Each section carries its own cost, plus labor, and by the time you have replaced half the door you may be spending a large share of what a new door would cost while keeping the older springs, hardware and insulation.

Age matters too. An older, uninsulated door with thin steel may not be worth investing in, especially if you heat the garage, use it as a workshop or have a room above it. A new insulated door can make a real difference in comfort through an Oconomowoc winter. If your current door is discontinued and no good match exists, a new door is often the only way to get a uniform look.

We give you a straight comparison. If one section makes sense, we will say so and price it. If a new door is the better choice, we explain why and show you options that suit your house, from simple raised-panel steel doors to carriage-house styles that fit the older lakefront homes and historic downtown streets. You make the call with all the information in front of you, and there is no pressure either way.

Installation Day

What Happens When We Swap A Section

Removing a section means working with a door that is held up by springs and cables. The bottom section is directly connected to the lift cables, so replacing it requires releasing spring tension in a controlled way and setting it again afterward. Upper sections involve unbolting hinges and the top brackets while the door is supported. None of this is a job for a homeowner, and a door that comes apart unexpectedly can fall with enough force to cause serious injury.

Once the damaged section is out, we inspect the hinges, rollers and brackets that came with it and reuse what is in good condition. Hardware that is bent, cracked or rusted gets replaced. The new section is set in place, hinges and brackets are mounted, and the door is reassembled. Because a new section can weigh slightly more or less than the old one, we rebalance the springs, check the cable tension on both sides and confirm the door sits level on the floor.

We finish by running the door through several cycles with the opener, checking the bottom seal for gaps, and testing the safety reversal. If the new section needs paint to match, we explain what to use and when it is ready to paint. The goal is a door that looks right from the street and works as well as it did before the damage happened.

Panel Replacement FAQ

Questions About Panel Replacement

Answers from the technicians who do this work across Oconomowoc every week.

Ask A Technician
01Can I replace just one panel of my garage door?

Usually, yes. Sectional doors are built so individual sections can be replaced, as long as a matching section is available for your model. We identify your door, check availability and confirm that the hinges and hardware will line up. If a match is not available, we explain the alternatives instead of installing something that will look out of place.

02Will a new section match the color of my old door?

The style and texture can match closely, but color is harder to predict. Doors fade over time from sun exposure, so a new section may look slightly brighter at first. Some homeowners accept a small difference, while others have the full door painted for a uniform look. We talk through this before ordering so there are no surprises when the new section goes in.

03What if my garage door model is discontinued?

Discontinued doors are common, especially on older homes. Sometimes a close match from the same manufacturer or another one will fit the hinges and look right. Other times there is no good option, and replacing the whole door is the only way to get a consistent appearance. We look into it before you spend anything and give you an honest answer.

04Is it cheaper to replace a section or the whole door?

Replacing one section on an otherwise healthy door is usually the less expensive choice. Once two or more sections need replacing, or the door is older and uninsulated, the numbers often start favoring a new door. We price both options so you can compare them side by side, and we quote the price before any work starts.

05Can I just hammer out a dent in my garage door?

Minor dents in steel doors can sometimes be improved, but trying to pound out a deep dent often stretches the steel, cracks the paint and makes rust more likely. Dents near hinges or the bottom bracket should be left alone because that area holds tensioned hardware. We recommend having a technician assess it before attempting any repair.

06Does a damaged section affect how my door works?

It can. A bowed or creased section may bind in the track, make the opener strain or reverse, and leave gaps at the floor. Damage near the bottom bracket can weaken the point where the lift cables attach. If your door is struggling or closing unevenly after an impact, it is worth having it inspected before the problem spreads to other parts.
