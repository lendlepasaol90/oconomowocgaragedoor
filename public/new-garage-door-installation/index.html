Getting It Right

Measuring Comes First And It Takes More Than a Tape

Most installation problems start before the new door ever shows up. The width and height of the opening matter, but so do the headroom above the opening, the sideroom on each side, and the backroom from the door to the back wall. Headroom decides which spring and track setup will fit. Sideroom decides whether the vertical tracks and their brackets have something solid to bolt into. Backroom tells us whether the horizontal tracks and an opener will clear the space without a custom layout.

We also look at things a quick estimate tends to skip. Is the floor level from one side to the other, or does the slab slope toward the drain? Older garages around Oconomowoc often have a floor that has settled an inch or more, which affects how the bottom seal meets the concrete. We check the jambs and header for rot, the condition of the framing above the opening, and whether there is a beam, duct, light fixture or storage rack in the way of the tracks.

All of that goes into the quote. If the jambs need new wood, if the header needs reinforcing for a heavier door, or if low headroom calls for a special track kit, you will hear about it up front, with the price, before anyone takes the old door down. No surprises halfway through the job.

Insulation Basics

What R-Value Means For a Garage Door

R-value is a measure of how well a material resists heat moving through it. Higher numbers mean more resistance. On garage doors it is usually reported for the section itself, so treat it as a useful comparison between doors rather than a promise about how warm the garage will be. Gaps at the bottom, sides and top, and how tight the section joints are, often matter just as much as the number on the label.

Doors generally come in three build styles. A single-layer door is one skin of steel with nothing behind it. A two-layer door adds a layer of insulation, often polystyrene panels, behind the steel. A three-layer door sandwiches insulation between two steel skins, and many of these use injected polyurethane foam that bonds to both skins. Foam-filled doors tend to carry higher R-values for the same thickness and also feel stiffer and quieter when they move.

Insulation is worth paying attention to if the garage is attached, if there is a bedroom or living space above it, or if you work out there in January. It also helps the door itself, because a stiffer, quieter door with a solid core resists dents better than an empty pan. For a detached garage used only for parking, a basic door may be all you need, and we will tell you that.

  • Single-layer steel: lightest, least expensive, no insulation
  • Two-layer steel: insulation backing, better sound and feel
  • Three-layer steel: insulation between two skins, stiffest build
  • Polyurethane foam: higher R-value per inch than polystyrene
  • Weather seals matter as much as the panel rating
  • Thermal breaks between sections cut down on frost lines

Install Day

Out With the Old In With Everything New

The first job on install day is safely releasing the tension on the old springs. That step is the reason a door replacement is not a weekend project. Torsion and extension springs hold a lot of stored energy, and letting it out takes the right winding bars and experience. Once the springs are unwound, we take the old sections down, pull the old tracks and hardware, and load it all up. We haul away the old door so it is not left sitting along the side of your house.

Then the opening gets prepped. We look at the jambs and header again with the old door gone, replace weatherstripping on the stop molding if it is worn, and make sure there is solid backing for the new brackets. The new sections go up one at a time, then the vertical and horizontal tracks, the spring assembly, cables and drums. New hardware means new hinges, new rollers, new bearings and new cables, sized for the weight of the new door.

Last comes the balance and adjustment. A properly balanced door stays put when it is lifted halfway and let go. We set spring tension for that, check that the door runs straight and quietly, set the bottom seal against the floor, and reconnect or install the opener. Before we leave, we walk you through the manual release and test the safety reversal with you watching.

What To Look For

Signs It May Be Time For a New Garage Door

Get A Free Quote

Rust Through the Bottom Section

Road salt tracked in on tires sits against the bottom panel all winter. Once rust eats through the steel skin, the section weakens and the seal stops working. Sometimes a single section can be replaced, but on older doors a full replacement often makes more sense.

Repairs Keep Adding Up

If you have paid for springs, cables, rollers and panel fixes over the past few years and the door is still giving you trouble, it is fair to compare the next repair with the price of a new door. We will give you both numbers honestly.

Cold Garage, Cold Room Above

An uninsulated door lets winter cold pour into an attached garage and up into the room over it. If the bonus room is the coldest space in the house every January, an insulated door with good seals can make a real difference.

Warped or Rotting Wood

Older wood doors absorb moisture from humid lake summers and snowmelt. Over time sections warp, joints separate and the bottom rots. When the wood is soft or sections no longer line up, refinishing will not bring it back.

Dents, Cracks and Damage

A bumper tap, a basketball or a hailstorm can crease steel panels. Small dents are cosmetic, but a creased section can bind in the track or crack at the hinges. If several panels are damaged, a new door is often the cleaner fix.

The Look No Longer Fits

After new siding, a new roof or a remodel, an old garage door can look out of place. Since it covers so much of the front of the house, replacing it is one of the most visible changes you can make to curb appeal.

Matching the House

A Door That Fits The Way Your Home Looks

The garage door can take up a big share of the front of a house, so the style choice deserves some thought. A lake home on Lac La Belle or Oconomowoc Lake often looks best with a carriage-house style door, either real wood or a wood-look composite or steel, sometimes with arched windows across the top section. Natural tones tend to sit well against cedar, stone and the trees around the water. Decorative strap hinges and handles finish the look without changing how the door works.

Newer homes at Pabst Farms usually have different needs. Many have three-car garages, sometimes a double door paired with a single, and taller openings that can be eight feet high or more for trucks and lifted vehicles. For those, we pay attention to how the doors line up with each other, matching panel styles across both openings, and spec the spring and track setup for the extra height and weight.

Historic homes near downtown, around Main Street and Wisconsin Avenue and the blocks close to Fowler Lake, call for something quieter. A flush or simple recessed-panel door in a muted color, or a carriage style with plain windows, keeps the garage from overpowering an older facade. On a detached garage behind an older house, we often find odd opening sizes, and we can order doors to fit.

Your Options

Choosing Windows, Colors And Little Details

Beyond material and insulation, a handful of smaller choices shape the finished door. Windows bring light into the garage and break up a large surface. They can sit in the top section only, which keeps privacy, or run down a side column on carriage styles. Insulated glass is a good match for an insulated door. Obscure or frosted glass lets light in without showing what is stored inside.

Color is the other big choice. Factory colors on steel and composite doors hold up better than paint applied later, and darker colors tend to show fading and heat more over many summers of direct sun. We bring samples and can talk through how a color will look next to your trim, siding and front door. Many homeowners match the garage to the trim. Others match it to the front door for a pulled-together look.

Then there are the practical details. Pinch-resistant section joints protect fingers. A stronger strut on the top section helps when an opener pushes against it. Low-headroom track kits help in older garages. If you are planning a new opener at the same time, it makes sense to install both together so the door and opener are matched and set up as one system.

Local Know-How

Oconomowoc Garages Come in Every Shape

Installing doors around Oconomowoc means working on almost every kind of garage. A detached garage behind a 1920s house near downtown may have a nonstandard opening, a sagging header and a floor that has settled toward one corner. A lake cottage on Okauchee Lake or Pine Lake might have limited headroom and framing that has seen decades of damp air. Subdivisions from the 1970s through the 1990s usually have standard openings but original doors near the end of their life. Pabst Farms homes often have tall, wide openings and multiple doors that need to line up. Add about 40 inches of snow, road salt and deep cold snaps, and the right door here is one with good seals, solid hardware and a finish that handles freeze-thaw cycles.

Garage Door Installation FAQ

Questions About Garage Door Installation

Answers from the technicians who do this work across Oconomowoc every week.

Ask A Technician
01How long does a new garage door installation take?

Most single-door replacements are finished in one visit, usually within part of a day. Larger doors, multiple doors, or jobs that need jamb or header repairs take longer. Custom sizes and special-order styles take time to arrive before install day. When we quote the job, we will tell you what to expect for both the order time and the time we will be at your house.

02Do you take away the old garage door?

Yes. Removal and haul-away are part of the job. We safely release the tension on the old springs, take the sections and tracks down, and load everything up so you are not left with a pile of steel or wood in the driveway. If the old opener is being replaced too, we take that as well. The quote will spell out what is included.

03Can I keep my existing opener with a new door?

Often, yes, if the opener is in good shape, sized for the new door's weight, and has working safety sensors. We check it during the estimate. A new insulated door is heavier than an old single-layer one, so an older, weaker opener may struggle. If the opener is very old, it can make sense to replace both at once, but we will not push it if yours is fine.

04Should I replace the springs and tracks or reuse them?

We install new tracks, springs, cables, rollers and hinges with a new door. Springs are matched to the weight and height of the specific door, so the old ones rarely balance a new door properly. Old tracks may be bent or worn and sized for a different door. Starting with new hardware gives the new door a fair start and avoids mixing worn parts with new ones.

05What R-value should I look for in an insulated door?

It depends on how you use the garage. For an attached garage with living space above it, or a garage you heat or work in, a three-layer door with polyurethane foam is usually worth considering. For a detached garage used mainly for parking, a lighter door may be enough. Keep in mind that good weather seals and a tight fit affect comfort as much as the panel rating.

06Can you install a door on an older detached garage?

Usually, yes. Older garages around town often have openings that do not match today's standard sizes, low headroom, or framing that needs work. We measure carefully, check the jambs and header, and can order doors in custom sizes. If framing repairs are needed to give the door something solid to mount to, we include that in the quote before starting.
