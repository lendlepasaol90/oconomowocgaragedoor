What Cables Do

Small Parts Carrying a Very Heavy Load

It is easy to overlook garage door cables because they are thin and tucked along the sides of the door. They do critical work. On a torsion system, each cable runs from a bottom bracket at the lower corner of the door, up the side of the track, to a grooved drum on the spring shaft. As the springs turn the shaft, the drums wind the cables and lift the door evenly from both sides.

On an extension spring system, the cables run through pulleys and connect to the springs and the track hangers, using a different path to do the same job. Either way, the cables are the direct link between the stored energy in the springs and the weight of the door. If one cable is weak, slack or out of position, the door can no longer lift evenly.

Because cables connect to parts that are under high tension, especially the bottom brackets, cable work carries real risk. A bottom bracket removed with the springs still wound can fly loose. Please leave cable repairs to a trained technician.

How Cables Fail

Fraying, Slipping and Snapping

Cables fail in a few predictable ways. The most common is fraying near the bottom bracket, where the cable sits closest to the floor. Moisture, snowmelt and road salt collect there, rust works into the strands, and individual wires start to break. Over time the cable thins until it can no longer carry the load and gives way.

Cables also come off their drums. This happens when a cable goes slack, often because the door hit something on the way down, the bottom seal froze to the floor, or a spring broke and the tension dropped. When tension returns, the loose cable can jump the drum groove and wrap incorrectly, or unspool entirely. The door then hangs crooked.

Worn drums, misaligned shafts and loose set screws can also cause cables to track poorly and rub against the door or track. That rubbing wears the cable quickly. When we replace cables, we look at why the old ones failed so the new ones do not meet the same end.

  • Rust and fraying at the bottom bracket
  • Cable jumping off the drum after going slack
  • Snapped cable from long-term wear
  • Cable rubbing on the door or track
  • Worn or cracked cable drums
  • Uneven cable tension from side to side

The Full Repair

More Than Just Swapping a Cable

A proper cable repair starts by securing the door so it cannot move, then safely releasing spring tension. We remove the old cables and inspect the parts they connect to. Bottom brackets are often as rusted as the cables, and a corroded bracket can fail even with a new cable attached. We check drums for cracks or worn grooves, the shaft for straightness, and the bearings at each end.

We replace cables in pairs. If one has frayed from salt and moisture, the other has almost always been exposed to the same conditions. New cables are cut and seated to equal length, so both sides of the door lift at the same rate. We then rewind the springs, verify that the cables are tight and centered in their drum grooves, and check that the door sits level on the floor.

Finally, we balance the door by hand, reconnect the opener, and run it through several cycles while watching the cables wind and unwind cleanly. We quote the price before work starts, including any brackets or drums that need replacing.

What To Look For

Signs Your Cables Need Attention

Get A Free Quote

Door Is Crooked When Closed

If one side of the door touches the floor while the other leaves a gap, or the door looks tilted as it moves, a cable has likely stretched, slipped, or broken. Stop using the door and call for an inspection.

Loose Cable Hanging Down

A cable dangling beside the track or lying on the floor is a clear sign it has snapped or come off its drum. The door may still be held by the other cable but is unstable. Keep clear and call for service.

Frayed or Rusty Strands

Look at the cables near the bottom corners of the door from a safe distance. Broken wires sticking out, reddish rust, or a section thinner than the rest means the cable is weakening and should be replaced before it fails.

Cable Bunched on the Drum

Cables should wind neatly into the grooves of the drums above the door. A cable that is piled up, overlapping, or sitting outside the grooves can slip off and drop one side of the door without warning.

Snapping or Twanging Sound

A sharp metallic twang during operation can mean a cable has jumped a groove or a strand has broken. It is different from the loud bang of a spring. Either sound means you should stop using the door and have it checked.

Door Jerks or Shudders

When cables have uneven tension, one side lifts before the other and the door jerks or shudders in the track. Over time this bends hinges and track sections. Leveling the cables early prevents more expensive damage to the door.

Related Problems

Cables Often Point to Another Issue

A failed cable is sometimes the cause of a problem and sometimes the result of one. A spring that has broken can let a cable go slack and jump its drum. A door that comes off its track can pull a cable out of position. A bottom seal frozen to an icy slab can stop the door while the opener keeps unwinding, leaving slack cable that tangles when the door finally breaks free.

That is why we never treat a cable call as only a cable call. We look at springs, track alignment, rollers and the opener's settings to make sure the conditions that caused the failure are corrected. If a spring is near the end of its life or a track is bent, we tell you, explain how it relates to the cable failure, and let you decide what to repair now.

Stay Safe

What to Do If a Cable Has Failed

If you see a loose cable hanging beside the door, or the door is visibly higher on one side, stop using it. Do not press the opener button again, since lifting a door on one cable can twist it out of the tracks. Keep people, pets and cars away from the opening and do not walk under the door.

Do not try to rewind a cable onto its drum or loosen the bottom bracket. Those parts are connected to wound springs. Call us instead. We offer same-day service when you call early, and we carry cables, drums and brackets for most residential doors in Oconomowoc and nearby towns, so the repair is usually completed in one visit.

Local Know-How

Salt, Snowmelt and Lake Air in Oconomowoc

The bottom corners of a garage door are where cables meet the floor, and in Oconomowoc that is a tough place to live. Winter brings around forty inches of snow, and cars carry slush and road salt from Highway 16, Highway 67 and the county roads straight into the garage. That brine pools at the door's edges and eats at cables and bottom brackets. Summer humidity off Lac La Belle, Fowler Lake and the other lakes keeps the steel damp. Freeze-thaw cycles also lock bottom seals to the slab, which can create slack cable when the opener runs. Rinsing salt away from the door edges and scheduling a fall inspection helps cables last longer in this climate.

Cable Replacement FAQ

Questions About Cable Replacement

Answers from the technicians who do this work across Oconomowoc every week.

Ask A Technician
01Can a broken garage door cable be fixed, or does it need replacing?

A frayed or snapped cable needs to be replaced, not repaired. Steel cable strands cannot be spliced safely to carry a door's weight. If a cable has only slipped off its drum without damage, it can sometimes be reseated, but we inspect it closely first. Any rust, kinks or broken strands mean replacement is the safer choice.

02Why do you replace both cables if only one failed?

Both cables are the same age and exposed to the same moisture and salt, so the second one is usually close behind. Replacing both also ensures they are equal in length and stretch, which keeps the door level. We quote the price before work starts so you can see the cost of one versus both.

03Is it safe to use my door with a frayed cable?

It is not a good idea. A frayed cable can snap without warning, dropping one side of the door and possibly pulling it out of the tracks. If you notice fraying, limit use of the door, keep everyone clear of the opening, and schedule a repair. We offer same-day service when you call early.

04What causes a cable to come off the drum?

It usually happens when the cable goes slack. Common causes include the door hitting an obstruction, a bottom seal frozen to the floor, a broken spring, or the door coming off its track. Worn drums and a misaligned shaft can contribute too. We correct the underlying cause so the new cable stays seated.

05Can I rewind the cable onto the drum myself?

Please do not. Cable drums and bottom brackets are connected to wound springs that hold a large amount of energy. Working on them without the proper tools and procedure can cause serious injury. Leave the door where it is, stop using the opener, and call for service.

06Do you also replace rusted bottom brackets?

Yes. Bottom brackets often rust along with the cables because they sit at floor level where salt and water collect. A badly corroded bracket can fail even with new cables attached. We inspect them on every cable repair and replace them when needed, with the cost included in the quote before work starts.
